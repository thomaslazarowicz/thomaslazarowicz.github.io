Working Papers

Demand-Driven Stagnation: Customer Acquisition and Firm Growth (Job Market Paper)

Peter Sinclair Prize for Best Paper (13th MMF PhD Conference)

Abstract: Do the effects of a demand shock on firm growth depend on which households it hits? I show that they do, because young firms rely disproportionately on low-income households for new customers. I use the UK’s 2010 welfare cuts as a demand shock concentrated at the bottom of the income distribution. Linking firm accounts to local exposure to the cuts, I find that young firms in more exposed areas cut spending on customer acquisition by up to 15% per standard deviation of exposure between 2010 and 2012. Older firms do not adjust. Employment, markups and capacity utilisation show no response, suggesting that firms build their customer base through spending on acquisition rather than through pricing. In a quantitative model of firm dynamics, low-income households end supplier relationships more often, so the pool from which firms acquire customers is poorer than the population, and young firms’ customer bases are poorer than old firms’. Who the shock hits then matters for aggregate outcomes. A shock of the same size borne by high-income households destroys about 30% less of young firms’ customer capital and costs £11bn less in lost output over the decade.

Monetary Transmission in a HANK Model with Housing and Rental Sectors, with Daniel Albuquerque and Jamie Lenney

Earlier version: CFM Discussion Paper 2025-29.
Previously circulated as “Monetary Transmission Through the Housing Sector”, Bank of England Staff Working Paper No. 1,115

Replication code

Abstract: Using new UK evidence, we show that a monetary tightening causes a hump-shaped fall in house prices while real rents rise. We explain these facts with a Heterogeneous Agent New Keynesian (HANK) model with housing, tenure choice and individual landlords. Tenure heterogeneity decouples rents from aggregate consumption, and behavioural frictions delay the fall in house prices. Because tenure is correlated with MPCs, rents and house prices account for a quarter of consumption’s monetary policy response, and a tightening redistributes from renters to landlords. Since tightening raises rents, policymakers should accommodate inflationary shocks that originate in the housing market.

Starting Over: Learning, Fresh Starts and the Reallocation of Serial Entrepreneurs

Abstract: Firms run by serial entrepreneurs are larger and more productive, yet little is known about how much aggregate output is lost because starting over is costly. I develop a firm-dynamics model in which entrepreneurs learn about their own ability and their business-specific match quality, and must liquidate and refinance to start over. I estimate the model on administrative data covering UK companies linked to their beneficial owners, which lets me follow entrepreneurs across the businesses they run. Two facts discipline the mechanism: within entrepreneurs, the serial productivity premium predates the switch to a new business, so it reflects selection on ability, not learning by doing; and exit rates fall with firm age, as entrepreneurs learn their ability in their first years. Because entrepreneurs learn slowly how good a match their business is, the most able wait years longer to start over than they would if they could see it. Removing the cost of starting over raises aggregate output by 2 percent and makes serial entrepreneurship the norm, with the gain coming from more firms, not better ones. For bankruptcy policy, recovering more of a failed firm’s capital does more for output than cutting the cost of a fresh start by a quarter.

Latent Demand Heterogeneity and Passthrough, with Federico Pessina and Dajana Xhani

Abstract: Most macroeconomic models of firm price-setting behaviour ignore household heterogeneity or impose a homothetic demand system that makes the distribution of customers irrelevant. Household consumption data, on the other hand, shows that households systematically consume different varieties, and their demand cannot be fully explained by the set of observables (income and demographics). We characterise own and cross-price elasticities with double CES preferences and heterogeneous households, and a finite number of firms in the lower nests. We show that the cross-household variance of expenditure shares can reverse the sign of the super-elasticity, turning incomplete cost pass-through into over-complete and flipping strategic price complementarity into substitutability. We estimate the joint distribution of tastes and elasticities by non-parametrically recovering latent heterogeneity from household scanner panel data, and compare the resulting pass-through with that of a representative-household model. Our results have implications for how firm-specific cost shocks, such as tariffs or exchange-rate movements, pass through to consumer prices.

Winners and Losers from Monetary Policy: Evidence from the UK Rental Market, with Morgane Richard

Abstract: This paper estimates the heterogeneous effects of monetary policy across a novel dimension, housing tenure. Using microdata from one of the UK’s largest property rental and sales websites, we show that, as in the US, a contractionary shock to the policy rate leads to 1) an increase in rental prices and simultaneously 2) a fall in house prices. The granular microdata also allows us to demonstrate that a contractionary shock leads to a decrease in the listing rate of rental properties, suggesting that this heterogeneous response across rents and house prices is not solely driven by household tenure decisions, but also by a rental supply channel. Finally, our identification approach does not lead to the typical ‘price puzzle’ found in many monetary VARs, suggesting that while contractionary policy lowers inflation, inflation incidence is heterogeneous across housing tenure and implies a new distributional channel of monetary policy.

Policy Papers

Household Debt and Labour Supply, with Phil Bunn, Jagjit Chadha, Stephen Millard, and Emma Rockall

Bank of England Staff Working Paper No. 941

Abstract: In this paper, we first develop a theoretical framework with three types of household: outright homeowners, mortgagors and renters. We then examine empirically how household debt affects the response of labour supply to shocks to income, mortgage interest rates and house prices for each type of household. In line with our framework, we find that negative income shocks lead to lower participation among outright homeowners while increasing mortgagors’ desired hours; surprise rises in interest rates lead to increases in desired hours that are larger the higher is the household’s debt level; and falls in house prices increase mortgagors’ desired hours.
